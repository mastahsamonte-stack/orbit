1 · The one philosophy

Everything below rolls up to a single sentence.

Value what it does TODAY, not what it could do. You underwrite the in-place, buyer-normalized NOI and offer on that. Upside (value-add) tells you how much room you have and how excited to be — it is never what you pay for. The seller wants to be paid for the upside you create; don't let them.

The whole valuation game is two numbers: NOI ÷ Cap Rate. Get the NOI honest and the cap rate defensible, and the price falls out. Most of underwriting is the discipline of lowering the seller's NOI to a number you can actually operate, and raising the cap rate to one the market and your risk justify. Both moves protect you.

From the recording "Buy what makes money on day one." The very first thing Pace says: don't buy a park that's losing money on day one unless you're advanced and have cash flow coming in from another park. His Beargrass RV resort in Montana throws off $13K/month free cash flow (after mortgage, private-money lenders, every expense, and a buffer) — that's what lets him stomach a park next door that's bleeding $5K/month. If you can't, you don't buy the loser yourself — you wholesale it to, or partner with, someone in the community who can. "Why grind two years to make $5K/month when you could just go find a park that makes $5K/month on day one?"
From the recording NOI is your "Play-Doh" — and "N-O means No, it's not your Income." NOI is the cash buyer's take-home (no bank, no private money, no debt). You are not a cash buyer, so NOI is never what you pocket — it's the lump of dough you get to play with to figure out how much debt you can carry. The mechanic, walked live: start with NOI → subtract annual bank debt service → what's left is split between a seller-carry payment, private-money-lender payments, and your actual take-home. "How did they structure that deal?" This is how: NOI is the Play-Doh, then you shape the debt around it.

2 · The 8-step process

First look → submitted offer. This is the spine; the rest of the guide is detail on each step.

1

Gather docs

P&L (2–3 yrs), T-12, rent roll / reservation report, OM (offering memo), tax returns. No real docs = you reconstruct income conservatively from the rent roll and bank deposits (and that under-management is the value-add).
2

Park basics

Site count & types, unit mix (monthly / weekly / nightly RV, MH pads POH vs TOH, other), infrastructure (city vs well/septic, electric metered or master), occupancy mix (transient vs long-term).
3

Market research

Demand drivers (employers, tourism, pass-through traffic, energy concentration risk), 5–8 competitors (rates, amenities, live Google ratings), and the market cap rate. Use the AI prompts A1–A4 here.
4

Run financials

Top-line trend, build a conservative NOI, adjust expenses UP for a new owner, and value it today (Adjusted NOI ÷ Market Cap).
5

Find value-add

Fill vacancy → raise to market rent → bill back utilities → then capex items. Cheapest levers first. Use AI prompts B1–B7.
6

Build pro forma

Year 1 = flat revenue + higher expenses (don't lease up while utilities are broken), then model the ramp, then ~3% growth after stabilization.
7

Choose financing

Know the seller's existing debt. Pick the structure: DSCR, seller carry, Morby Method, or lease-option.
8

Send the offer

LOI that protects both sides. If the deal is overpriced, lead with terms, not a low price. ("When price won't work, terms can.")

3 · The numbers that matter

The formulas

WhatFormula
NOIIncome − Operating Expenses
Value todayAdjusted NOI ÷ Market Cap
Implied capAsking ÷ Adjusted NOI
Quick NOIRevenue × 50% (conservative)

Rule of thumb: implied cap below 8% = overpriced for an RV park. Every $1 of durable NOI ≈ $11 of value at a 9 cap (1 ÷ 0.09).

The cap-rate trick

No clean RV comp? Anchor to apartments:

Nearest metro's suburban C-class apartment cap rate + 4 points.

RV parks usually land 8–10% (~9%). Lean to the higher end for single-industry, tertiary or small markets — a higher cap = a lower, safer price.

From the recordingWhy the trick exists: commercial parks lack the sale-comp volume single-family has, so you anchor to the market cap rate (the broker should know it). Credited to Dustin Kercher. On Odessa, suburban C-class + 4 → ~9%, which exposed the 6% asking cap as overpriced.

Adjust expenses UP for a new owner — the four that move the needle

LineWhy it goes upHow to set it
Property taxes the big oneReassessed on sale — almost always higher than the seller's frozen basisModel the reassessed amount at your purchase price, not their old bill
Labor / managementA "free-rent on-site manager" understates real cost — and many owners pay them a reduced cash fee in exchange for free lot rent, so it barely shows in the P&LFrom the recording: ~5% of revenue for on-site management + a 3% management fee = ~8% all-in if owner-run. (On the Odessa deal Pace bumped a $26K "contractor" line up to $40K (~5%) plus the 3% fee, rather than trust the seller's number.)
InsuranceHardening market; trailing number is staleGet a real quote; expect a modest bump
ReservesLenders require it; pads/roads/hookups wear outSet cash aside per pad per year

This is why the seller's NOI is almost always optimistic. Your normalized NOI is the one you offer on.

From the recording The "no payroll" tell — and how to weaponize it. A suspiciously low or missing payroll line usually means a mom-and-pop owner lives on site and "does nothing," paying themselves nothing — so the numbers look too good to be true. Worse, giving a manager free rent instead of a paycheck quietly dodges employment taxes. Once you buy, someone has to run it, so that cost is real and it craters the NOI. Use it in the negotiation: "I see how you're handling on-site management — I used to do the same until I got audited, so I'll have to pay real payroll, which raises my expenses…" Underwriting is the magic wand for negotiating — every sharp question both lowers the defensible price and builds your credibility as a serious buyer.

4 · The spreadsheet engine, tab by tab

The evaluator has 38 tabs, but four do the real work. Here's how they wire together so you can drive it — or rebuild it — with confidence.

① Inputs & Analysis — the heart

Everything starts here. You type the property, the rent mix, utilities, and additional income; the seller's NOI and implied cap fall out automatically.

Utilities & BillbackElectric included? Meters installed? Monthly electric cost, billback %Potential monthly/annual billback income (only counts if not already in rent and no individual meters)
Additional IncomePropane, laundry, storage, other (monthly)Total ancillary income

The T-12 sanity block. Alongside the tidy input section, the tab carries the seller's real EOY / YTD / T-12 figures so you can tie your reconstructed income back to actual collections. Always tie out — if your model can't reproduce the trailing income within a few percent, something's wrong (see prompt B7).

② Comps Analysis — the market & the pro forma

You drop in 5–12 competitor parks (rent, occupancy, billback Y/N, amenities, site count, rental type). It averages the market by rental type and builds the Pro-Forma NOI from market rent × market occupancy, with a target expense ratio (default 50%, "includes mgmt & payroll") and 3% annual rent growth. This is where your value-add upside becomes a dollar figure.

③ Financing — offer scenarios & DSCR

Runs your offer at multiple cap rates (asking, 8%, 9%, 10%, custom) under two structures and reports debt service, DSCR (as-is & pro-forma), cash-on-cash, and equity created:

  • SBA loan — e.g. 30% down, 8.5%, 25-yr amortization, target DSCR 1.25.
  • Seller finance — e.g. 30% down, 4%, 30-yr, 5-yr balloon, interest-only toggle. Also layers a PML (private-money) line to model the down payment being financed — i.e. the Morby stack.

DSCR = NOI ÷ annual debt service. Lenders want ≥ 1.25. Notice seller-finance DSCRs run far higher (2–3×) because the rate is lower and often interest-only — that's why creative terms let you pay more and still sleep.

④ Deal Summary — the one-screen verdict

Pulls it together: current vs pro-forma income/NOI/cap, price-per-site, a recommended offer (pro-forma NOI ÷ target cap), down payment, expected DSCR & Year-1 CoC, and a decision matrix:

CriteriaTarget
Cap rate (on your offer)≥ 8%
DSCR≥ 1.25
Cash-on-cash, Year 1≥ 15%
Market occupancy≥ 85%

6 · Financing & cash-to-close

The four structures

StructureHow it works
DSCR loanUnderwritten on the property's NOI, ~70% LTV. Low NOI = low appraisal — so your normalized NOI directly caps your leverage.
Seller carrySeller finances part of the price; bridges the gap between bank debt and price.
Morby MethodSenior DSCR loan + seller carry in 2nd-lien position (often low/0% interest, balloon). Appraisal still applies.
Lease + optionLease now, buy later — buys you time to raise NOI before you pay for it.

Morby Method, mechanically

A DSCR lender takes first position (~75–80% of value) based on the income; the seller carries the remaining ~20–25% as a second-position note, frequently at a low or 0% rate with a long or no balloon. Structured well, seller credits can approach your closing costs — minimizing cash in.

Why it's powerful: the bank's risk is collateralized and the seller is motivated, so you assemble most of the capital stack without a big check — provided the appraisal (i.e. the NOI) supports it.

Cash to close is NOT just the down payment. Budget: Down payment + CapEx + Reserves (≈6 mo) + lender debt-service reserve. Goal: a credible path to $10K+/month cash flow within ~1 year of stabilizing.

7 · Value-add levers (cheapest first)

Because the asset is valued on income, every durable $1 of NOI ≈ $11 of value at a 9 cap. Size each lever in both NOI and value, and rank by return on capital.

#LeverMechanicsCapital
1Fill vacant padsLease up the pads that already have working utilities — pure NOI, near-zero cost$
2Raise rents to marketThe biggest mom-and-pop gap. Size it: (market rent − in-place) × occupied pads × 12$
3Recover utilitiesSub-meter electric (~90% recovery, needs meter capex) or RUBS allocation (~80%, no meter capex — but check state legality). Water/sewer billback alone can add $50–$100/pad/mo.$$
4Add ancillary incomeLaundry, storage, RV/boat storage pads, propane, vending — high margin on existing dirt$$
5Infrastructure / expansionRepair offline pads, add sites on owned land — biggest upside, most capital & risk$$$
Sequencing rule from the cheat sheet: in Year 1 hold revenue flat while you fix utilities (no lease-up until pads can actually be rented), absorb offline pads at a realistic pace (e.g. 3–6/month), then grow ~3% after stabilization. Optimism in the ramp is where models lie.

8 · The AI underwriting playbook

The mentor's exact workflow for Steps 3 & 5. Fill the brackets, run in order, each prompt feeds the next. AI extracts and researches; you set conservative assumptions and verify every number.

Six rules before you run anything: (1) Feed it real data — actual P&L, rent roll, occupancy. (2) Make it cite every number with source + date. (3) Tell it to be conservative and flag every assumption. (4) Verify the math yourself — AI transposes and invents. (5) One clean folder per deal. (6) Tie the model back to actual collections.

Part A — Market research (Step 3)

A1 · Market drivers & demand

# Produces: market narrative + risk register
You are an experienced RV park underwriter. Research
the market for [CITY, STATE] and tell me whether
demand for monthly RV and workforce housing is
strengthening or weakening. Cover the main economic
drivers (employers, industries, tourism, pass-through
traffic), population/job trends, and single-industry
concentration risk. If it's an energy market, include
oil price forecast and the break-even price where
drilling slows. Cite every source with a date. End
with a plain-English verdict on demand + top 3 risks.

Tip: for energy/tourism markets, ask for the one leading indicator (rig count, oil price, visitor counts) to watch after you buy.

A2 · Competitor comp set (live ratings)

# Produces: Comp Set + Amenities Matrix
Find 8–12 RV parks within [25] miles of [CITY/ADDRESS]
that compete for monthly & workforce tenants. For each
pull: name, city, distance, size in pads, monthly rate
(+daily/weekly if posted), utilities included vs billed
separately, amenities, and the LIVE Google rating &
review count. Put it in a table. Mark anything you
can't confirm as 'call to confirm' and list sources.
Then tell me where [subject park] sits on price and
rating vs the set.

Review counts reveal who the established operators are. Pull ratings live from Maps, not memory.

A3 · Cap-rate benchmark

# Produces: your market cap range
What's a defensible going-in cap rate for an RV park
in [CITY, STATE] with [single-industry / tertiary]
characteristics? Give national RV averages, the state
average, and the [100–249] pad-size bracket — each with
source + date. Recommend a conservative going-in range
for THIS asset and explain the risk adjustment. Also
run the shortcut: nearest metro's suburban C-class
apartment cap rate + 4 points.

Lean high for single-industry/small markets. Higher cap = lower, safer price.

A4 · Rate positioning & bill-back

# Produces: Rate & Bill-Back tab; first levers
Using the comp set: (1) Is [subject park] under- or
over-priced vs comps? (2) Are competitors billing back
electric or including all utilities, at what all-in
rate? (3) To recover utility costs, must we cut base
rent, or does the market tolerate rent-plus-electric?
Quantify the annual revenue impact of moving our rents
to market at current occupancy.

This is the bridge from Step 3 into Step 5 — the rent gap is upside in dollars.

Part B — Value-add & the pro forma (Step 5)

B1 · Extract financials from the PDFs

Here are the seller's docs [upload P&Ls, rent roll,
occupancy]. Extract into clean tables: combined
line-item P&L by year with totals, NOI, expense ratio;
rent roll by unit type with posted & effective rents;
monthly occupancy series. Flag inconsistencies between
docs, any expense line missing/too low, anything booked
at zero. Cite which file each number came from.

Catch the tells: $0 property tax, no payroll line, two conflicting P&Ls. Hunt for what's missing.

B2 · True stabilized occupancy

From occupancy reports & rent roll, separate pads with
working utilities from offline pads. Over a full year,
what occupancy do the pads that CAN be rented actually
run? Use that as conservative stabilized occupancy —
not a guess. Tell me how many pads have been dark, for
how long, and the historical absorption pace.

This single move makes the model credible — stabilized = how working pads actually perform.

B3 · Normalize the seller's NOI

Re-underwrite the seller's NOI to a buyer-normalized
number. Add/true-up: market payroll & on-site mgmt, a
3% management fee, realistic insurance, R&M, reserves
per pad, and FULL property tax after a conservative
reassessment (not the seller's frozen value). Show
seller NOI and normalized NOI side by side, each
adjustment with its basis.

The real number is almost always lower than the seller's. Make it justify each line so you can defend it.

B4 · Identify & size the levers

Given the asset & comp set, list every value-add lever
and size each in annual NOI and the capital required.
Start cheapest (fill pads, rents to market, recover
utilities), then capital-heavy (infrastructure,
expansion). For each, give annual NOI impact and value
created at a [9%] cap. Rank by return on capital.

Every $1 durable NOI ≈ $11 at a 9 cap — translate each lever into both NOI and value.

B5 · Multi-scenario pro forma

Build a 10-year operating pro forma (no debt, no sale)
on 4 scenarios: Conservative, Realistic, Upside,
Rents-to-Market. Hold occupancy flat during the repair
window, then absorb offline pads at [3/4/6]/month to
each scenario's stabilized occupancy. Grow income &
expenses [3%] after stabilization. Step property tax up
on reassessment. Output in-place, Y1, Y2, stabilized
Y5, Y10 NOI + stabilized value at an [8–10%] cap range.
Make every assumption an editable input on one tab.

Offer on the Conservative case. The other three show how much room you have.

B6 · Operating-model variants

Add alternative operating models as SEPARATE scenarios,
not stacked: (1) slow-build to market rent (new tenants
at market, existing convert at year 3); (2) sub-metered
electric bill-back (~90% recovery, meter capex); (3)
RUBS allocation (~80% recovery, no meter capex). Compare
stabilized NOI and tell me which captures the gap to
market most efficiently and best shifts utility-inflation
risk to tenants.

These are alternative paths to the same goal, not additive.

B7 · Expense-ratio decomposition & tie-out QA

Decompose the stabilized expense ratio and explain why
it is what it is (an all-bills-paid park runs high). Show
the specific levers that move it toward 50% (rents to
market, utility recovery, tax protest) and quantify each.
Then sanity-check: does in-place income reproduce actual
collections within a few percent? Flag anything that
doesn't tie out.

Always end with a tie-out. If the model can't reproduce the seller's actual collections, something is wrong.

Special situations From the recording

No operating history (newly built)

A brand-new park has no real P&L to underwrite. So you build the income from comps: secret-shop 3+ competing parks and have AI do what people used to do by driving around. Claude can open each competitor's booking site and read its live, seasonal, dynamic nightly/weekly/monthly rates across the whole calendar, then estimate your achievable rate — typically within ~95% of reality.

The car-and-clipboard market survey, automated. Validate it, but it's your starting income.

A park that's in the red

Find the buyer before you underwrite. A money-loser is a specific buyer's deal — "the capability of your buyer makes the deal work, not the spreadsheet." Line up 4–5 buyers who can stomach the loss and raise the capital, then structure it: a low cash offer or their full price on a long fuse (take the loan sub-to, fund the turnaround with private money, refi in ~12 months to repay lenders, take no distributions for ~2–3 years).

Don't get obsessed with one lead: "The offer is the offer." Send it, follow up every 2 weeks, move on to the next.

9 · Re-using the model for MHP & Multifamily

Pace's point: the same engine values any income asset, because the core is always NOI ÷ Cap Rate. The spreadsheet is even named the "MHP / RV Park Evaluator" and already carries MH-pad rows. Here's what stays and what changes.

What's identical

  • NOI ÷ cap valuation
  • Normalize expenses up (taxes, mgmt, insurance, reserves)
  • Buy on in-place, not pro-forma
  • Conservative / Realistic / Upside scenarios
  • DSCR / seller-carry / Morby financing
  • Value-add = fill, raise to market, recover utilities

Mobile Home Parks

  • Use the MH-pad rows: POH (park-owned homes — more income but more expense/headache) vs TOH (tenant-owned — sticky, low-cost, preferred).
  • Watch utility infrastructure (city vs private water/septic) — same billback/RUBS levers, bigger dollars.
  • Cap rates a touch tighter than RV (~7–9% stabilized; 9–10%+ for value-add) — longer-term, stickier tenancy = less risk.

Multifamily

  • Swap "pads/sites" for units; rent mix becomes unit types (1BR/2BR).
  • Lower cap rates (national MF ~5%+) → higher prices; creative terms matter even more.
  • Add vacancy & bad-debt loss, turn/make-ready costs; RUBS for water/trash is the classic lever.
  • Expense ratios typically run higher than RV; reserves & capex (roofs, HVAC) loom larger.
One mental model, three assets: reconstruct honest in-place NOI → divide by a defensible, risk-adjusted cap → that's today's value. Everything asset-specific is just which income lines and which expense lines you populate. The discipline never changes.

10 · Golden rules & red flags

Golden rules

  • Value what it does today, not what it could do.
  • Buy what makes money on day one — don't buy a loser unless you're advanced with cash flow from another park (else wholesale/partner it).
  • NOI ÷ cap rate is the whole valuation game. NOI is your "Play-Doh," not your take-home.
  • Always run conservative / realistic / upside — offer on conservative.
  • When price won't work, terms can — every offer can include a full-asking-price option if structured their way.
  • Make an offer on every park; expect a "no" and be happy to move on. "The offer is the offer."
  • Be a deal detective — underwriting is the magic wand for negotiating. Aggressive on sending offers; methodical & conservative on underwriting.
  • Write down every question — it's a conversation, not a one-shot.

Red flags / tells

  • $0 or frozen property tax in the P&L (it will reset).
  • No payroll/management line (owner working free).
  • Two conflicting P&Ls; income that won't tie to deposits.
  • Master-metered utilities with no sub-meter path.
  • Single-industry/one-employer demand (esp. energy).
  • Offline pads sold as "occupancy upside" before utilities are fixed.
  • Implied cap under 8% on the seller's own (optimistic) NOI.

12 · One-page underwriting checklist

Before you model

  • Got T-12, 2–3 yr P&L, rent roll, occupancy, tax returns?
  • Site count & mix confirmed (RV mo/wk/nt, MH POH/TOH)?
  • Utilities mapped: city vs well/septic, metered vs master?
  • Seller's existing debt known?

Market (AI A1–A4)

  • Demand verdict + top-3 risks, sourced & dated?
  • 8–12 comps with live Google ratings?
  • Defensible cap range (and C-apt + 4 check)?
  • Rent gap to market quantified in $?

Normalize & value (AI B1–B7)

  • Property tax reassessed to my basis?
  • Market payroll + 3% mgmt fee added?
  • Insurance re-quoted, reserves per pad set?
  • Stabilized occupancy = working-pad reality?
  • Normalized NOI shown beside seller NOI?
  • Model ties to actual collections (B7)?

Offer

  • Priced on conservative NOI ÷ target cap?
  • Cap ≥8% · DSCR ≥1.25 · CoC ≥15% · occ ≥85%?
  • Cash-to-close incl. capex + reserves budgeted?
  • Terms ready if price is rejected (Morby/seller carry)?
  • LOI protects both sides?
